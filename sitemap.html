Information architecture

Organized around the request, not features.

The navigation follows the life of a request in a small company: someone asks for money, approvers decide, the accountant pays out from the company's funds, and receipts settle what was advanced.

Primary objectRequest
Primary actorsTeam lead and accountant
Cross-cutting layerRules, roles and activity trail

03.02

Navigation rationale

Each top-level item is a job someone in the company does, not an internal system object.

Asking and deciding

Requests

Create, approve, reject and pay out advances, reimbursements and vendor payments. The hero workflow.

Closing the loop

Settlements

Receipts that settle an advance, and money spent out of pocket. Reviewed by the accountant.

Where money sits

Funds

The two bank accounts and petty cash that payouts come from.

Who can do what

Team

Members and roles that decide who asks, who approves and who pays out.

Looking back

Reports

Spend by department and type, and how much was paid as advances.

Control layer

Settings

Approval rules that decide which chain a request follows.

03.04

Cross-cutting architecture

Some capabilities belong to every request, so they are not separate destinations.

TRAIL

Activity trail

Every change records who, when, the old and new state, and the rule that applied.

RULES

Approval logic

Rules are written in Settings but shown wherever they change a request's chain.

ROLES

Permission model

Roles change the available actions while keeping enough context to hand over.

03.05

IA guardrails

Rules that keep the product legible as more request types are added.

01

Keep work near the request

Approving belongs inside Requests, not in a generic “Tasks” inbox that strips the amount and the rule.

02

Show the rule where it bites

People should see why the director was added at the moment they submit or decide.

03

Do not hide recovery

Rejected, cancelled and failed requests stay findable with their history.
